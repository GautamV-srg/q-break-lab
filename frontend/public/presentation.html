The problem

Encrypted traffic recorded today can be opened later

  • Harvest now, decrypt later. An adversary stores ciphertext today and waits for the hardware.
  • Shor's algorithm breaks RSA and elliptic curves at any key size; Grover's halves the strength of every symmetric key.
  • Most organizations have never seen a quantum attack on their own messages, so the risk stays abstract and migration stalls.

Enterprises

Customer data, contracts, internal traffic protected by AES and RSA.

Governments

Communications that must stay secret for decades.

Secure-messaging providers

Showing users, with evidence, how their encryption holds up.

Quantum approach · symmetric

Grover's search on MiniAES, with the cipher inside the oracle

  • The oracle runs the cipher reversibly, in circuit, for every key in superposition, then cleanly uncomputes.
  • Three attack modes: known beginning, known substring at an unknown position, and ciphertext only.
  • Quantum counting estimates how many keys match before the search, so the iteration count is derived, not guessed.
  • Every measured key is re-checked classically before anything is reported.
Quantum approach · public-key

Shor's period finding on MiniRSA, down to one counting qubit

  • Moduli built from distinct odd primes up to 11: 15, 21, 33, 35, 55, 77.
  • Three constructions, cross-checked to give the same factors: textbook swaps, permutation blocks, and iterative phase estimation.
  • The iterative circuit reuses a single counting qubit in place of a 2n-qubit register and inverse QFT.
  • Disclosed: the modular-multiplication blocks are permutations computed classically when the circuit is built. The period is read from the quantum measurement.
Why quantum, here

These are the two problems where quantum changes the answer

Key search is unstructured search

Classically nothing beats trying keys: about N/2 tries. Grover needs about √N oracle calls, and that is provably optimal. This is the quantum threat to symmetric encryption.

Factoring hides a period

RSA's security is the difficulty of factoring N. Shor turns factoring into finding the period of aˣ mod N, which a quantum Fourier transform reads off in polynomial time.

Same circuits, small numbers

The oracle, diffuser, phase estimation and post-processing are the ones a full-scale attack would use. Only the sizes are miniature, because the simulator's memory doubles with every qubit.

Two blind classical baselines

Trial division, and classical order finding: counted in divisions and modular multiplications.

  • Every Breach Report carries a Quantum vs classical box built from that run's comparison record.
  • For N ≤ 77 a handful of trial divisions factors N in microseconds. The simulator takes milliseconds to seconds.
  • What the miniature run demonstrates is the workflow and its resource counts: the part that scales polynomially where classical factoring does not.
Noise analysis

On noisy hardware, even the miniature breach fails

  • Depolarising noise is added to every gate; we sweep the error rate and re-run the attack.
  • Success falls to random guessing at error rates far below what today's devices achieve.
  • Fake IBM backends (real chip layouts and calibrations) tell the same story.
  • In the app, the noise lab in Pop the Hood re-runs your own breach under noise, live.

Takeaway. Real breach testing needs fault-tolerant, error-corrected quantum hardware. It does not exist yet.

Depth and problem size

Qubits grow gently. Depth, and the simulator's memory, do not.

The 16-bit wall. 16-bit keys are deliberately not simulated: about 30 qubits means a state vector of roughly 16 GB, with about 200 Grover iterations over it. That limit is the point: classical simulation of quantum circuits does not scale.

Limitations · rules of engagement

What this prototype does not claim

  • Miniature ciphers. MiniAES is an AES-inspired toy (4-bit block, one round). MiniRSA uses real RSA arithmetic on tiny moduli.
  • Classical simulator. Genuine Qiskit circuits, executed on Qiskit Aer. No quantum hardware.
  • It does not break real AES or RSA. Real key sizes need large fault-tolerant machines.
  • No speed-advantage claim. The simulation is slower than the classical baselines.
  • Disclosed pre-computation. Shor's multiplication blocks are classically computed permutations.
  • Ciphertext-only is often ambiguous. Many keys can fit; the report says so instead of picking one.
  • Noise is fatal today. Ideal-simulator results do not transfer to current hardware.
  • Self-generated instances. Keys, messages, moduli and seeds are generated by the project and reproducible.
